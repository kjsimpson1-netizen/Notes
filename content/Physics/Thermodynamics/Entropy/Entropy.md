This is closely related to the [[2nd Law of Thermodynamics]], as in it has to do with the degree of randomness of a substance, and the spread of energy. Entropy adds a quantitative measure of this randomness that we perceive. We can represent it with the following equation for only [[Adiabatic]] processes:
$$\begin{align*}
\Delta S&= \frac {dQ}T\\
\hline\\
Q&= \text{Heat Flow}\\
T&= \text{Temperature (Kelvin)}
\end{align*}$$
We can see that this is true, because adding the same amount of $Q$ when the temperature is very low will have a massive change in entropy (e.g. a liquid to a gas). Whereas if we added the same amount of heat to a hotter object, the relative change in entropy. 
### [[Isothermal]]
The equation for the change in entropy of any isothermal process is:
$$\begin{align*}
\Delta S&= nRT\ln\left(\frac{V_f}{V_i}\right)
\end{align*}$$
### [[Isochoric]]
The equation for the change in entropy of any isochoric process is:
$$\begin{align*}
\Delta S&= nC_V\ln\left(\frac{T_f}{T_f}\right)
\end{align*}$$
### [[Isobaric]]
The equation for the change in entropy of any isobaric process is:
$$\begin{align*}
\Delta S&= nC_P\ln\left(\frac{T_f}{T_i}\right)
\end{align*}$$


### Entropy in Cyclic Processes
Since the change in entropy is invariant of path, if we end up at the same place that we initially started at, the change in the entropy of the working substance will be 0. 
## Microscopic Interpretation.
This is similar to the idea of calculating all the individual kinetic energies of molecules in a gas to give it's total internal energy. However, unlike internal energy, entropy is something that belongs to each of the individual molecules, and rather it is something that belongs to the system as a whole. 
This relies on the idea that the most likely state of the gas is to be exactly at average, as flipping 100 coins will most likely end up with 50 heads and tails. This is because the macroscopic state with 100 coins (the macroscopic state) having 50 heads and 50 tails has the most ways that the individual coins (microscopic states) can be arranged and have this even split. Since this also therefore has the highest amount of randomness with where the 100 microscopic states can have the same macroscopic state, it also therefore has the highest entropy. 
We can represent this same idea with entropy and gas molecules using the following equation:
$$\begin{align*}
S&= k\ln w\\
\\
k&= \text{Boltzmann's Constant}\\
w&= \text{Number of Arrangements}
\end{align*}$$
This is also why even though that it is possible that all the gas molecules in a room will move to one side and leave the other in a vacuum (called free compression), this is next to impossible. Using the coin analogy, this is like every gas molecule landing on heads simultaneously.
