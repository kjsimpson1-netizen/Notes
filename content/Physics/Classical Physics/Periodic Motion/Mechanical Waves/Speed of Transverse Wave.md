This has to do with any type of [[Mechanical Waves]] that have their motion perpendicular to the propagation of the wave, such as light, sound and even guitar strings. For the guitar strings example, the speed of this wave depends upon the tension in the string, as well as the density of the string per unit length ($\mu$). 
## Longitudinal Velocity
For example, if we pick up a string off the ground, the velocity of the string leaving the ground ($v_y$) is constant, as well as the speed of the point of string that is in contact with the ground. We can then derive an equation for the speed at which this point moves along the ground, being:
$$
\begin{aligned}
v&= \sqrt{\frac F\mu}\\
\hline\\
v&= \text{Velocity}\\
F&= \text{Force}\\
\mu&= \text{Density per Unit Length}
\end{aligned}
$$
## Transverse Velocity
This is the speed of propagation of the wave through the medium, and this is determined by mechanical properties of the medium. This is not the same thing as the speed of the particles when they are disturbed by the wave. 
Another thing to note is that the medium itself does not actually move through space, rather it is just the wave, and a wave will only transport energy, and not matter from one region to another. 
We can calculate the wave speed with:
$$
\begin{aligned}
v&= \lambda f\\
\hline\\
v&= \text{Velocity (m/s)}\\
\lambda &= \text{Wavelength (m)}\\
f&= \text{Frequency (Hz)}
\end{aligned}
$$
If we change the wavelength or the [[Frequency]], then the velocity that the wave propagates must remain the same.
