This is the warning of what force will be placed upon a positive [[Electric Charge]] placed in the field. The SI units of an electric field are $N/C$. These are used to pre-calculate forces. (If there is no [[Electric Charge]], then there is no force).
$
$
\begin{aligned}
\vec{F_e}&= q\vec{E}\\
\hline\\
F&= \text{Force}\\
q&= \text{Charge}\\
E&= \text{Electrical Field}
\end{aligned}
$
$
## Electric Potential
We can also relate the electric field to the [[Electric Potential]] through the fact that the derivative of the [[Electric Potential]] will be the negative of the electric field, which can be found from the [[Potential Gradient]].
$
$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla \vec E(r)=V(r)
\end{aligned}
$
$
## Point Charge
The equation for the electric field is the same as the equation for [[Coulomb's Law]] between [[Electric Charge]], however without one of the two charges. $
$
\begin{aligned}\\
\vec{E}&= \frac{kq_1}{r^2}\hat d\\
\hline\\
E&= \text{Electric Field}\\
k&= 9.00\times10^{9}\\
q&= \text{Charge}\\
r&= \text{Radius}
\end{aligned}
$
$
>[!Note]
There can be a superposition of electric fields, and these fields will add up as vectors. We can express this as:
$
$
\begin{aligned}
\vec E_T&= \sum \vec E
\end{aligned}
$
$

## Electric Field Lines
These are the imaginary lines that show the direction that the force will be directed at that point. If there is a high electric field in a location, then the lines will be close together, and if the density is low then the lines will be far apart. These lines will point away from positive [[Electric Charge]] and towards negative [[Electric Charge]].
>[!Note]
>These electric field lines can never intersect and are always perpendicular to the surface of a [[Conductor]]
