 The general expression for the electrical potential energy is given by:
$$\begin{align*}
U&= \frac{kq_1q_2}r=qV\\
\hline\\
U&= \text{Potential Energy}\\
k&= \text{Electric Constant}\\
q&= \text{Charge}\\
r&= \text{Radius}\\
V&= \text{Electric Potential}
\end{align*}$$
## Charge in a uniform Field
Electrical potential energy is the electrostatics equivalent of gravitational potential energy to a mass. If there is a lower potential energy, then the charge is in a more stable state than if the charge has a higher potential energy. We can say that the work done by moving an [[Electric Charge]] is equal to the change in potential energy, or:
$$\begin{align*}
W_{a\rightarrow b}&= U_a-U_b=-\Delta U\\
\hline\\
W&= \text{Work}\\
U&= \text{Potential Energy}
\end{align*}$$
If the [[Electric Charge]] is moved with the electric force then the electrical potential increases, and vice versa for moving opposite the electric force.
## Multiple Charges
The same principle applies as with one charge with respect to a point charge, however with each charge divided by it's distance to the reference point being summed. This results in the following equation:
$$\begin{align*}
U&= kq\sum_i\frac{q_i}{r_1}\\
\hline\\
U&= \text{Potential Energy}\\
k&= \text{Electric Constant}\\
q&= \text{Charge}\\
r&= \text{Radius}
\end{align*}$$
Therefore if we do this at the two points and then take the difference, this will result in the amount of work required to move the charge. 
## Force
We can also relate this to the equation for [[Coulomb's Law]] to find the potential:
$$\begin{align*}
\Delta U&= -\int\vec F\cdot d\vec s\\
\hline\\
U&= \text{Potential Energy}\\
F&= \text{Force}\\
s&= \text{Position}
\end{align*}$$
Therefore, the force is equal to the negative gradient of the potential energy:
$$\begin{align*}
\vec F&= -\nabla U\\
\hline\\
U&= \text{Potential Energy}\\
\vec F&= \text{Force}
\end{align*}$$
