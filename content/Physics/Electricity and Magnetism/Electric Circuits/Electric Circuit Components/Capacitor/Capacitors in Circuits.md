## Transient and Steady States
A steady state is when a circuit has been sitting there for an infinite amount of time (in practice, 5 time intervals) When it is in a steady state, there will be no more flow of [[Current]] through the circuit. Therefore, the capacitor will act like perfect [[Dielectric]]. 
 A transient state is when there is a change in the state of the circuit. When this occurs, a cap of a [[Capacitor]] will act like a wire and will have very low [[Resistance]]. 
## DC Circuits
### Capacitors in Parallel
Unlike with [[Resistor]] wired in  where the overall [[Resistance]] is less than any one resistor, with capacitors, the more capacitors wired in make a larger [[Capacitance]].
$
$
\begin{aligned}
C_T&= \sum_{i=1}^n C_i
\end{aligned}
$
$
### Capacitors in Series
This is the opposite of [[Resistor]] in series again. This is "increasing" the gap between the two end caps, and this will cause there to be less overall [[Capacitance]]. The two caps of each capacitor will always have the same charge. However, two capacitors wired in will always have less [[Capacitance]] than either one
$
$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{C_T}&= \sum\limits\frac1C
\end{aligned}
$
$
## Capacitors in an AC Circuit
Capacitors in an [[Alternating Current]] circuit follows the same principles as the [[Inductor]], however with [[Capacitive Reactance]]. The [[Voltage]] of a capacitor lags behind the [[Current]] by $90\degree$, and can be expressed as:
$
$
\begin{aligned}
V_C&= IX_C\\
\hline\\
V_C&= \text{Voltage Amplitude}\\
I&= \text{Current Amplitude}\\
X_C&= \text{Capacitve Reactance}
\end{aligned}
$
$
>[!Note]
>Since the [[Current]] constantly switches, the capacitor will always act as a wire since it is as if there has been a switch that just opened.
