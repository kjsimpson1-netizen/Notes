There is a direct proportional relationship between the [[Electric Field]] and the [[Current Density]], and the quotient of the two is the resistivity of the material, measured in Ohm-meters ($\Omega\cdot m$):
$
\begin{aligned}
\rho&= \frac EJ\\
\hline\\
\rho&= \text{Resistivity}\\
E&= \text{Electric Field}\\
J&= \text{Current Density}
\end{aligned}
$
The greater this resistivity, the greater the electric field needed to cause a given [[Current Density]]. A perfect conductor has 0 resistivity and a perfect insulator has infinite resistivity.
We can also express the resistivity by the amount of free electrons present, and the time between collisions:
$
\begin{aligned}
\rho&= \frac m{ne^2\tau}\\
\hline\\
\rho&= \text{Resistivity}\\
m&= \text{Electron Mass}\\
n&= \text{Free Electrons}\\
e&= \text{Magnitude of Charge}\\
\tau&= \text{Average Time between Collisions}
\end{aligned}
$
## Temperature
When the temperature of a material is increased, there is also an increase in the resistivity, as the molecules in the medium will move around more, causing more collisions with the electrons. We can express the resistivity as a function of temperature:
$
\begin{aligned}
\rho(T)&= \rho_0[1+\alpha(T-T_0)]\\
\hline\\
\rho&= \text{Resistivity}\\
T&= \text{Temperature}\\
\alpha&= \text{Temperature Coefficient of Resistivity}
\end{aligned}
$
