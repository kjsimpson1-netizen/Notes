Leibniz created his notation by thinking of the derivative like finding the [[Slope]] of a line, with a small change in $y$ denoted by $dy$ and a small change in $x$ being denoted in $dx$. Therefore the slope will be small changes in $y$ over small changes in $x$, and given by the expression:
$$\begin{align*}
\text{Derivative}&= \frac{dy}{dx}
\end{align*}$$
## Higher Order Derivatives
Leibniz is useful for any higher order [[Derivative]], as it can be represent by:
$$\begin{align*}
\text{Higher Order Derivative}&= \frac{d^{(n)}y}{dx^{(n)}}\\
\\
\text{Order of the derivative}&= n
\end{align*}$$
## Evaluating the Derivative
When we want to evaluate the derivative at a point, we draw in the evaluation line as shown:
$$\begin{align*}
\frac{dy}{dx}\Big|_{x=a}&\text{ or }\frac{dy}{dx}(a)
\end{align*}$$
This is the derivative evaluated at the point $x=a$.
## Antiderivative
For taking the antiderivative, Leibniz thought of it as summing up all the areas of the infinitesimally small points on the graph, and therefore used the symbol as it is the "sum":
$$\begin{align*}
\text{Antiderivative}:\int f(x)
\end{align*}$$
