This was actually invented by Euler, but popularized by Lagrange. This used the prime symbol to show the [[Derivative]], or a $'$ mark.
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Derivative}&= f'(x)
\end{aligned}
$$
## Higher Order Derivatives
For higher order derivatives, there are more prime symbols added to the function, but after the 3rd derivative, it becomes difficult to see, so we can show it as either an exponent (in either lower case roman numerals or Arabic numerals in parenthesis):
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Higher Order Derivatives}: \begin{cases}
f^{(4)}(x)&\text{Arabic}\\
f^{iv}(x)&\text{Roman}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$
## Evaluating the Derivative
When evaluating the derivative using Lagrange's notation, we just treat it like a normal function:
$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(a)
\end{aligned}
$$
This is for the function $f(x)$ evaluated at $x=a$.
## Antiderivative
For showing the antiderivative, Lagrange used the idea that the antiderivative is like taking the [[Derivative]], but backwards. Therefore he showed the antiderivative as a negative:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Antiderivative}: f^{(-n)}
\end{aligned}
$$
>[!Warning]
>This can be easily confused with the notation for [[Inverse Functions]] for the first antiderivative
>$$
\begin{aligned}
f^{(-1)}(x)&\neq f^{-1}(x)
\end{aligned}
$$
