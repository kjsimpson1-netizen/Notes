This will tell us the [[Spontaneity]] of a given reaction, and it is expressed with the following equation relating [[Chemistry/Entropy/Entropy|Entropy]] and [[Chemistry/Thermodynamics/Enthalpy]], as well as temperature:
$
$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G&= \Delta H-T\Delta S\\
\hline\\
H&= \text{Heat}\\
T&= \text{Temperature (Kelvin)}\\
S&= \text{Entropy}
\end{aligned}
$
$
If the Gibb's free energy is less than 0, than the reaction is spontaneous, and if it is positive then the reaction will not occur (however, if it is in [[Chemistry/Equilibrium/Equilibrium|Equilibrium]] then the reverse reaction will be spontaneous). If the Gibb's free energy is 0, then the reaction is at [[Chemistry/Equilibrium/Equilibrium|Equilibrium]].
## Non-Standard Conditions
If the reaction is occurring under non standard conditions, we can express this in terms of the [[Chemistry/Equilibrium/Reaction Quotient]]:
$
$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G_\text{rxn}&= \Delta G^o+RT\ln Q\\
\hline\\
G^o&= \text{Standard Free Energy}\\
R&= \text{Ideal Gas Constant}\\
T&= \text{Temperature}\\
Q&= \text{Reaction Quotient}
\end{aligned}
$
$
## Equilibrium
If the reaction is at [[Chemistry/Equilibrium/Equilibrium|Equilibrium]], then we can express this with the the standard Gibb's energy as 0 to give us:
$
$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G\degree&= -RT\ln K\\
\hline\\
R&= \text{Gas Constant}\\
T&= \text{Temperature}\\
K&= \text{Equilibrium Constant}
\end{aligned}
$
$
We can then combine the two previous equations to get:
$
$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta G_\text{rxn} &= RT\ln\left(\frac QK\right)\\
\hline\\
R&= \text{Gas Constant}\\
T&= \text{Temperature}\\
K&= \text{Equilibrium Constant}\\
Q&= \text{Reaction Quotient}
\end{aligned}
$
$
