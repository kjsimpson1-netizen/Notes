## How it was found
It is very hard to measure an atomic radius because the electrons are [[Chemistry/Basic Chemistry/Orbitals]] and not [[Chemistry/Basic Chemistry/Orbits]] as shown in the [[Chemistry/Basic Chemistry/Bohr Model]]. Scientists found this number by calculating the length of [[Chemistry/Atomic Structure and Properties/Bond/Covalent Bonds]], and then using this distance in diatomic ions ($O_2,F_2$) and then dividing it by 2, as both of the two elements are identical and will have their identical bond length. The units for sub-atomic lengths are called Angstrom ($\mathring{A}$). 
## How it changes
#### Across a period
As we got across a period of the [[Chemistry/Basic Chemistry/Periodic Table]], the [[Chemistry/Coulomb Forces]] will increase as there are more protons with the same amount of [[Chemistry/Basic Chemistry/Electron Shells]], therefore the [[Chemistry/Basic Chemistry/Valence Electrons]] will feel a greater [[Effective Nuclear Charge]] and the **atomic radius will decrease**.
#### Down a group
As we go down a group of the [[Chemistry/Basic Chemistry/Periodic Table]], the [[Atomic Radius]] will increase as there are more [[Chemistry/Basic Chemistry/Electron Shells]]. There are also more protons so the [[Chemistry/Coulomb Forces]] will increase, but the extra shells have a greater impact than the greater pull from the [[Chemistry/Coulomb Forces]].

#### Cationic Radii
For any given element, it's cation will have a smaller [[Atomic Radius]] than the neutral species. This is because with fewer electrons, there is less repulsion between the electrons. Also, in some cases, if the last of the [[Chemistry/Basic Chemistry/Valence Electrons]] are removed, we lose an entire electron shell.
#### Anionic Radii
The opposite is true here, as the anion always has a larger atomic radius than the neutral species. This is because there is a larger repulsion between the electrons as there are more of them packed into the same shell.
